---
title: "Menu matrix methodology"
description: "How MenuWright classifies menu items using sales mix and cost-aware profitability signals with the Kasavana–Smith method."
---

The menu matrix is MenuWright's core model. Every menu item is placed using two axes — **popularity** (menu mix) and a profitability signal: contribution margin for items with usable food costs, or a revenue-share proxy for items without them. The method follows the classic Kasavana–Smith menu engineering approach (1982), the industry-standard way to classify items by what they earn and how often they sell.

## The two axes

**Popularity — menu mix percentage**

```
menu mix % = item quantity sold / total quantity sold
```

**Profitability — contribution margin**

effective unit contribution margin = effective unit price − food cost

When a sales record has positive quantity and revenue, effective unit price is
`gross revenue / quantity sold`; otherwise the analysis uses the menu price.
Thus discounts reflected in sales revenue can change the margin from the menu
price shown on the menu.

## The thresholds

An item is "high" on an axis when it clears that axis's threshold:

| Axis | Threshold |
|---|---|
| **Popularity** | `1 / N × 0.70`, rounded to 0.01 (with a 0.01 floor). `N` is the number of active menu items. |

The profitability threshold is the quantity-weighted average contribution
margin for cost-known items. It uses all items in full-cost mode and only the
cost-known subset in partial-cost mode. Values are rounded to cents; an item
at or above its applicable threshold counts as high.

With no cost data, the engine uses each item's gross-revenue share as a
profitability proxy; its cutoff is the quantity-weighted average of those
shares, not a contribution-margin threshold.

## The four quadrants

| | High popularity | Low popularity |
|---|---|---|
| **High profit** | **Star** — protect it: keep quality consistent, give it prime placement, don't discount. | **Hidden Gem** — promote it: better placement, rename, or a bundle. |
| **Low profit** | **Crowd Pleaser** — reprice it: raise price or trim portion cost. It sells well but earns thin margin. | **Leftover** — cut or reinvent it: low sales, low margin. |

These are MenuWright's current product labels. The classic literature calls the same quadrants Star, Plowhorse, Puzzle, and Dog; the product renamed them to be self-explanatory to restaurant owners.

## Cost-data coverage and fallback

When every item has a usable food cost, all items are classified using their
contribution margins and sales mix.

With **partial** cost coverage, items that have costs use margin-based
classification and a threshold computed over only those items. Items without
costs receive a **provisional** revenue-share classification. To interpret
every item's profitability as contribution margin, enter costs for all active
items.

When **no** item has a food cost, the profitability axis uses revenue share as
a proxy while popularity still uses sales mix. This can identify relative
sales patterns, but it does not provide contribution margins or a true
profitability classification.

## Analysis snapshots

Every analysis run writes an immutable `MenuAnalysis` snapshot with per-item results. Snapshots let you:

- Track how classifications change between periods — was that item always a Leftover, or did it use to be a Star?
- Compare trends month over month

## What happens after classification

Classification is only the first half. After a run, MenuWright's AI layer reviews the matrix and produces specific, prioritized recommendations — reprice, promote, bundle, or cut — each with a projected monthly dollar impact where one can be estimated. Recommendations attach to the analysis snapshot and appear in the dashboard's Recommendations feed.

The AI generates candidates; you decide what to act on. When you accept a recommendation, MenuWright anchors a closed-loop measurement: it compares the item's average daily revenue in matched calendar windows before and after the action day and, once both windows have at least 7 days of recorded sales, displays the observed revenue change alongside the original projection. This is an honest before/after comparison, not an attributable impact — see the [FAQ](/menuwright/faq) for the full explanation.
